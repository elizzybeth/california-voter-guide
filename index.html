How to read this guide

Vote meanings and fiscal estimates come from the official guide. Campaign arguments are attributed, not adopted. “Read past the pitch” and interest assessments are editorial reasoning. Uncertain outcomes stay uncertain.

This guide does not infer your politics or score your alignment. Each measure presents competing values so you can make your own assessment. It covers statewide measures only and is not an official government publication.

READ THESE TOGETHER

40 ↔ 41 ↔ 42

Three measures, one potential collision. Proposition 40 creates a wealth tax. Proposition 41 restricts spending-limit exemptions; Proposition 42 restricts new asset taxes and retroactivity.

The legislative analyst says that if 41 or 42 receives more Yes votes than 40, courts could find a conflict and prevent 40 from taking effect even if it passes. This is a possible legal outcome, not an automatic prediction. Read the analysis ↗

PROP01

Housing

Housing, with a repayment plan

Borrow now to fund affordable housing and veterans’ housing, or preserve future budget capacity?

+ A YES vote

Authorize $11.25 billion in housing bonds.

− A NO vote

Reject this bond authorization; existing housing programs are not repealed.

The case for

US Vets, Habitat for Humanity CA and the CA Federation of Teachers argue that financing can accelerate affordable homes and prevent homelessness.

The case against

No opposition argument was submitted to the official guide. The substantive tradeoff is debt service and competing budget needs; absence of an argument is not consensus.

Public cost & revenue Estimated repayments: $500–600 million annually for about 25 years.

Read past the pitch Editorial assessment

The campaign’s ‘no new taxes’ language does not mean free. Bond repayments use public resources. Funding homes also does not guarantee any particular reduction in rents.

Money & interests

Yes donors include Airbnb ($1m) and OpenAI ($500,000). FPPC disclosure ↗

Inference: housing providers and construction workers can gain funded projects and jobs; residents can gain homes. A shared benefit does not establish misuse.

The values in tension

Housing access and investment today ↔ debt capacity and flexibility tomorrow.

A question to weigh, not a voting recommendation.

PROP02

Taxes & budgets

A larger rainy-day fund

How much should the state save during good years, and how should those savings interact with spending limits?

+ A YES vote

Change reserve and debt-payment rules to build larger reserves and extend extra debt payments.

− A NO vote

Keep current reserve and debt-payment rules.

The case for

CA Professional Firefighters, the LA Chamber of Commerce and Gavin Newsom support a larger cushion for services during downturns.

The case against

Assemblymember David Tangipa argues that excluding reserve deposits from the spending limit makes taxpayer rebates less likely.

Public cost & revenue Higher reserves, with less flexibility to spend or return some money immediately.

Read past the pitch Editorial assessment

Saving money does not create new revenue. ‘Protect services’ is a purpose, not a guarantee against cuts; ‘more spending’ does not erase the larger reserve requirement.

Money & interests

No donor figures verified for this measure.

Inference: service providers value reliable budgets; taxpayers may value rebates. These are policy stakes, not evidence of personal enrichment.

The values in tension

Stability across recessions ↔ present-day spending choices and taxpayer refunds.

A question to weigh, not a voting recommendation.

PROP03

Taxes & budgets

Keep the high-income tax

Should the existing higher income-tax rates expire or become permanent?

+ A YES vote

Keep the current high-income tax increase permanently.

− A NO vote

Let it expire in 2031 under current law.

The case for

California Teachers Association, California State PTA and Planned Parenthood Affiliates of CA argue that continuation protects education and health funding.

The case against

California Taxpayers Association, Family Business Association and California Hispanic Chambers of Commerce argue that a temporary tax should end and spending should improve first.

Public cost & revenue Maintains an estimated $5–15 billion a year after the scheduled expiration.

Read past the pitch Editorial assessment

‘No tax increase’ compares rates with today. Compared with scheduled law after 2031, affected taxpayers would pay more. A No vote does not end this tax immediately.

Money & interests

Yes: CTA Issues PAC contributed about $60.5m. FPPC disclosure ↗

Inference: education workers and institutions benefit from funding; affected high earners benefit from expiration. Neither interest alone settles the merits.

The values in tension

Progressive revenue and public services ↔ honoring sunsets and limiting ongoing tax burdens.

A question to weigh, not a voting recommendation.

PROP04

Democracy

Allow public campaign funding

Should governments be allowed to design programs that publicly fund candidates?

+ A YES vote

Lift the ban, subject to restrictions on eligible funds, candidates and uses.

− A NO vote

Keep the ban for state and most local governments.

The case for

League of Women Voters of CA, California Nurses Association and Consumer Watchdog argue public financing can reduce dependence on large donors.

The case against

California Taxpayers Association and Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association object to using taxes for campaigns and warn about program costs.

Public cost & revenue A few hundred thousand dollars annually for state advice and administration; future program choices matter.

Read past the pitch Editorial assessment

Permission is not a statewide funding program. The measure neither eliminates private political spending nor automatically establishes unlimited payments to candidates.

Money & interests

No donor figures verified for this measure.

Inference: qualifying candidates could receive public money; taxpayers fund programs. Claims that every opponent is protecting donor power go beyond that evidence.

The values in tension

Broader access to campaigning ↔ opposition to subsidizing political speech with taxes.

A question to weigh, not a voting recommendation.

PROP05

Democracy

Separate recall from replacement

Should a recall ballot also choose the replacement?

+ A YES vote

Remove the replacement question; use succession, appointments or later elections depending on the office.

− A NO vote

Keep the same-ballot replacement contest.

The case for

League of Women Voters of California and California Common Cause argue that replacements should not win with a small plurality.

The case against

Brian W. Jones and Joe Patterson argue voters lose their immediate choice of successor and politicians gain appointment power.

Public cost & revenue Net costs or savings are unknown and depend on the recall and office.

Read past the pitch Editorial assessment

This does not abolish recalls. It does remove an immediate electoral choice. Guaranteed savings are not established by the fiscal analysis.

Money & interests

No donor figures verified for this measure.

Inference: parties and officeholders face different succession incentives. That is an institutional stake, not proof of a financial payoff.

The values in tension

Orderly succession and majority legitimacy ↔ direct, immediate voter choice of replacement.

A question to weigh, not a voting recommendation.

PROP37

Housing

Loans for new-home buyers

Use a bond-funded mortgage program to help eligible middle-income households buy newly built homes?

+ A YES vote

Create the program with up to $25 billion in revenue bonds repaid by borrowers.

− A NO vote

Do not require this new assistance program.

The case for

United Nurses Associations of California, California Conference of Carpenters and Treasurer Fiona Ma argue it expands homeownership without taxpayer repayment.

The case against

No opposition argument was submitted. Questions remain about eligibility, who can afford the remaining purchase price, and how much assistance translates into more construction.

Public cost & revenue No direct state or local cost in the official summary; borrowers repay loans.

Read past the pitch Editorial assessment

This is a loan, not a free down payment. It targets qualified new homes and eligible buyers, not all renters or existing homes. ‘No taxpayer risk’ is broader rhetoric than ‘no direct cost.’

Money & interests

Yes: Realtor-sponsored Homeownership for Families contributed about $18.8m. FPPC disclosure ↗

Inference: real-estate sales and construction can benefit alongside buyers.

The values in tension

Access to ownership and new construction ↔ targeting help toward households with the greatest need.

A question to weigh, not a voting recommendation.

PROP38

Health

Borrow for immune-system research

Commit long-term public financing to a specific research field and funding structure?

+ A YES vote

Authorize $8.4 billion in research bonds.

− A NO vote

Reject these bonds; other medical research funding can continue.

The case for

Michael J. Fox Foundation, Alzheimer’s Los Angeles and American Nurses Association/CA support research toward treatments and prevention.

The case against

Researcher Robert M. Kaplan argues the debt and earmarks reduce flexibility and research should compete through the budget process.

Public cost & revenue $500–600 million annually for roughly 20 years, potentially offset by research revenues.

Read past the pitch Editorial assessment

Cures and self-financing are uncertain. ‘Designed’ to avoid taxpayer cost is not a guarantee. Half the bond funding goes to one UC-affiliated nonprofit institute, an important concentration of resources.

Money & interests

Yes: Gary Michelson contributed $45m directly. FPPC disclosure ↗

Inference: recipient institutions gain funding and influence over research priorities. Philanthropic support is not evidence that a donor personally profits.

The values in tension

Public science and long-term discovery ↔ open funding competition and budget flexibility.

A question to weigh, not a voting recommendation.

PROP39

Democracy

Add voter identification requirements

Add identification steps to voting, balancing verification and access?

+ A YES vote

Require government-issued identification in person and identifying numbers on mail-ballot envelopes.

− A NO vote

Keep signature-based identification under existing rules.

The case for

The Transparency Foundation and Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association argue identification improves trust in elections.

The case against

ACLU California Action, Common Cause and League of Women Voters of California argue the rules create unnecessary barriers for eligible voters.

Public cost & revenue Tens of millions to low hundreds of millions of dollars annually.

Read past the pitch Editorial assessment

Popularity does not establish how much fraud a rule prevents. Existing signature verification is not ‘no verification.’ Opponents’ partisan labels do not quantify how many eligible voters would be blocked; that outcome is not established here.

Money & interests

Yes: Richard Uihlein $17m. No donors include the California Democratic Party. FPPC disclosure ↗

Inference: participation rules create political incentives on both sides. The reviewed evidence does not establish a direct commercial payoff.

The values in tension

Additional verification and confidence ↔ accessible voting, privacy and administrative cost.

A question to weigh, not a voting recommendation.

PROP40

Taxes & budgets

A one-time billionaire wealth tax

Raise substantial temporary health funding from wealth while accepting collection and behavioral uncertainty?

+ A YES vote

Enact a one-time 5% wealth tax on qualifying billionaires.

− A NO vote

Do not enact this wealth tax.

The case for

Bernie Sanders and SEIU United Healthcare Workers West argue billionaires should contribute more to health care.

The case against

California Primary Care Association, California School Boards Association and California Taxpayers Association warn about fiscal and economic consequences.

Public cost & revenue Tens of billions in temporary revenue; possible ongoing income-tax losses below $1 billion annually, per the analyst.

Read past the pitch Editorial assessment

A one-time receipt cannot permanently solve an annual funding gap. Conversely, claims that everyone is directly taxed misdescribe this measure’s billionaire scope. Exact collections and behavioral responses remain uncertain.

Money & interests

Yes: SEIU-UHW. No: Building a Better California contributed $158.5m. FPPC disclosure ↗

Inference: taxed asset owners have a direct financial stake; health workers and providers have a funding stake. Opposition also includes service organizations.

The values in tension

Redistribution and health access ↔ predictable taxation, durable revenue and economic uncertainty.

A question to weigh, not a voting recommendation.

PROP41

Taxes & budgets

Audits—and limits on new taxes

Require audits while restricting new taxes that bypass the state spending limit?

+ A YES vote

Add pre-ballot and recurring audits, and restrictions on spending-limit exemptions for new taxes.

− A NO vote

Keep existing audit responsibilities and spending-limit rules.

The case for

California Society of CPAs, CalAsian Chamber of Commerce and California Taxpayers Association argue audits improve accountability.

The case against

Bernie Sanders and SEIU-UHW argue the measure is designed to obstruct the billionaire tax.

Public cost & revenue Net fiscal effect unknown; audits cost money and any resulting savings depend on later decisions.

Read past the pitch Editorial assessment

An ‘audits’ description omits a major tax restriction. Audits do not guarantee better outcomes. The potential conflict with Prop. 40 is substantive, but the courts’ eventual interpretation is not certain.

Money & interests

Yes: Building a Better California $58.35m. No: SEIU-UHW-backed committee. FPPC disclosure ↗

Inference: people exposed to new wealth taxation benefit from restrictions; service funders may lose options.

The values in tension

Oversight and enforceable spending rules ↔ flexible revenue tools and voters’ ability to fund services.

A question to weigh, not a voting recommendation.

PROP42

Taxes & budgets

Prohibit new asset-ownership taxes

Constitutionally restrict future personal-property taxation and certain retroactive taxes?

+ A YES vote

Prohibit new state taxes on owning financial assets and other personal property, with retroactivity limits.

− A NO vote

Preserve those future taxing options; this vote itself imposes no new tax.

The case for

CA Professional Firefighters, State Building & Construction Trades Council and AMVETS Department of CA emphasize protecting savings.

The case against

Bernie Sanders and SEIU-UHW argue the measure protects billionaires from Prop. 40.

Public cost & revenue Could reduce future revenue relative to what otherwise might be enacted; amount unknown.

Read past the pitch Editorial assessment

Protecting savings is broader than the immediate billionaire-tax dispute. The official analysis says simply owning financial assets is not currently taxed; a No vote is not a new retirement-account tax. The ban would extend beyond Prop. 40.

Money & interests

Yes: Building a Better California $159.1m. No: SEIU-UHW-backed committee. FPPC disclosure ↗

Inference: asset owners gain protection from future taxes; government loses an option.

The values in tension

Tax predictability and property protection ↔ future flexibility to tax accumulated wealth.

A question to weigh, not a voting recommendation.

PROP43

Democracy

A higher bar for local special taxes

Should voter-initiated local special taxes require a majority or two-thirds approval?

+ A YES vote

Require two-thirds approval for covered taxes beginning January 1, 2027.

− A NO vote

Keep majority approval for these voter-proposed taxes.

The case for

California Taxpayers Association, Family Business Association and California Hispanic Chambers of Commerce argue taxes should require broader consent.

The case against

California Professional Firefighters, California Federation of Teachers and Nurse Alliance of SEIU CA argue the change obstructs funding for essential services.

Public cost & revenue Future local tax revenues may grow less than under current rules.

Read past the pitch Editorial assessment

This does not itself cut existing taxes. A minority can block a tax supported by a majority; whether that is protection or obstruction is the underlying value dispute, not a factual disagreement.

Money & interests

Yes: California Business Roundtable PAC $11.64m. No: SEIU committees. FPPC disclosure ↗

Inference: taxpayers and businesses gain veto protection; publicly funded services and workers face a higher funding hurdle.

The values in tension

Majority rule and local services ↔ supermajority consent for new tax burdens.

A question to weigh, not a voting recommendation.

PROP44

Health

A spending floor for community clinics

Should covered nonprofit clinics have to devote 90% of revenue to program services?

+ A YES vote

Set the 90% requirement with penalties for covered clinics.

− A NO vote

Do not impose this new spending requirement.

The case for

The official pro campaign contact is SEIU-UHW. Supporters argue the rule redirects money toward clinics’ charitable mission and restrains executive spending.

The case against

American Academy of Pediatrics CA, California Medical Association and California Academy of Family Physicians warn of closures and lost services.

Public cost & revenue State administration in the low tens of millions annually, covered by fees.

Read past the pitch Editorial assessment

Program services include more than direct patient care. A ratio alone does not demonstrate care quality. The claim of hundreds of closures is a campaign prediction, not a verified outcome in the summary.

Money & interests

Yes: SEIU-UHW committee. No: AltaMed about $6.13m. FPPC disclosure ↗

Inference: regulated clinics have a direct operating stake; the union has a workforce stake. Neither establishes that every claimed patient benefit or harm will occur.

The values in tension

Spending accountability ↔ operating flexibility and continuity of safety-net care.

A question to weigh, not a voting recommendation.

PROP45

Housing

Faster reviews, narrower challenges

Speed up covered projects by changing environmental review and court procedures?

+ A YES vote

Apply new deadlines and narrower court-review procedures to eligible projects.

− A NO vote

Retain existing project-review and court-challenge rules.

The case for

California Children’s Hospital Association, California Water Association and California Council for Affordable Housing argue faster construction reduces costs.

The case against

Coalition for Clean Air, CA Environmental Voters and National Wildlife Federation warn about weaker review and public protections.

Public cost & revenue Initial annual government costs in high tens of millions, possibly over $100 million; longer-term effects uncertain.

Read past the pitch Editorial assessment

Faster approval does not guarantee lower household bills. Keeping environmental standards while narrowing enforcement can still change practical protection. Predictions of no affordability benefit are also stronger than the fiscal evidence establishes.

Money & interests

Yes: building-industry and utility donors. No: Wendy Schmidt $10m. FPPC disclosure ↗

Inference: builders and utilities can gain time and lower delay costs; communities bear project impacts. Financial interest does not alone disprove a supply benefit.

The values in tension

Housing and infrastructure delivery ↔ environmental enforcement and meaningful public review.

A question to weigh, not a voting recommendation.

SOURCES & LIMITS

Evidence before certainty.

Campaign claims are not findings

The Secretary of State publishes arguments from advocates alongside independent analysis. Their presence in an official booklet does not validate them. This guide flags omissions and unsupported certainty rather than treating all disagreements as equally factual.

Supporter and opponent lists are selected examples, not exhaustive endorsements. “No argument submitted” does not mean no opposition exists. Fiscal estimates are forecasts, not guarantees.

What the money tells us

Donors shown are selected examples from FPPC disclosures checked November 3, 2026, not campaign totals. Lists have reporting thresholds. Transfers and committees covering multiple measures can overlap; do not add figures across measures. Missing figures do not mean zero fundraising.

Donations establish support, not motive or corruption. Interest assessments are explicitly labeled inferences. This is a dated research snapshot, not a live finance tracker.
